Weeks 1 to 5 made one origin fast. A faster origin does nothing for a user in Mumbai whose data sits in Virginia. Light in glass crosses that gap in 63 ms, so one question and its answer cost 126 ms before your code runs. No CPU removes that wait.

This class rents somebody else's computers, in more than 300 cities, and puts them in front of your origin. The first half buys time back. The second half is the surprise. All your traffic already passes through those computers, so they became your firewall, your rate limiter, your flood absorber, your login check, and a place to run your code. The bill arrives at the end, in trust.

**The path through this read.** Part 1 measures the floor that distance sets. Part 2 says what a CDN is, and how a fleet of caches agrees on who owns which URL. Part 3 reads the cache: what the edge keeps when you say nothing, the eight words it reports, and four tricks that cache a page which looks personal. Part 4 breaks that cache two ways, then moves the same bytes faster. Part 5 turns the edge into a security perimeter. Part 6 runs your own code up there, and reads the bill.

<Picture breaks="a helper who does not know the answer is still useful, because she keeps a line to your friend's house open. Opening a line takes several trips of its own, and that is the part the picture hides.">

Your friend lives on the far side of a big city. You shout a question down a very long tube to her house. The tube carries your voice at a fixed speed, so one question and its answer cost two minutes, even when she answers at once. Now put a helper in a shop near your house. When she knows the answer, you get it in seconds. When she does not, she shouts down the same tube and you wait the full two minutes.

The tube is the fibre, and the fixed speed is light in glass. Your friend is your origin, and the helper in the shop is the edge. The answers the helper already knows are the cache. The walk to the shop is the 10 ms to the edge.

</Picture>

<KeyIdea id="the-pool-saves-handshakes-the-cache-saves-the-trip" title="The pool saves handshakes, only the cache saves the trip">

An edge with a warm connection to your origin removes the setup round trips, which takes 621 ms down to 233 ms. It cannot remove the 194 ms leg, because the bytes still come from the origin. A cache hit deletes that leg completely, and the answer arrives in 34 ms.

</KeyIdea>

Light in a vacuum travels 299,792 km each second. Glass has a group index near 1.468, so light in fibre travels 204,218 km each second, which is 4.90 ms for each 1000 km one way. Mumbai to Ashburn is 12,847 km on the great circle, so the round-trip floor is 126 ms. A new TLS 1.3 connection then needs three of those round trips to the first byte, about 380 ms of nothing. Mumbai to Chennai is 1,033 km, a floor of 10 ms against a measured ping of 23 ms. That ratio of 2.3 is routing, not physics, and the Internet median sits near 3.2.

Distance charges you twice, because it raises the loss rate as well as the round trip. The Mathis formula says one flow carries about its segment size divided by the round trip, divided again by the square root of the loss rate. The Akamai table of <SlideLink session={6} page={10} /> walks it out: 44 Mbps under 100 miles, and 0.4 Mbps at about 6,000 miles. The far edge is 110 times slower while it is only 60 times further away. One TCP flow cannot fill a long path, which is why multiplexing and BBR both exist.

<KeyIdea id="a-cdn-rents-geography-not-technology" title="A CDN rents geography, not new technology">

Every part of an edge came from an earlier class. What you cannot install yourself is the geography: a cache 10 ms from your user, both ends of the long leg under one operator, and enough cities that one address answers everywhere.

</KeyIdea>

A CDN edge is the reverse proxy of class 4 with its three usual jobs. It terminates TLS, it caches, and it inspects requests. The cache is `proxy_cache`, the warm pool back to your origin is `upstream keepalive` from class 3, and the one address that answers in every city is anycast, from class 2. Squid did reverse-proxy caching in 1996, so even the combination is old. What a CDN sells you is geography.

Inside one city many cache machines share the work, so something has to pick an owner for each URL. That is the founding question of a CDN, and `hash(url) % N` answers it badly. N sits inside the formula, so a change to N changes almost every owner, and the bench moves 90.9% of 100,000 URLs when ten caches become eleven. Even load is not the problem, because modulo leaves the busiest server at 1.02 times the mean. The churn is the problem, and it arrives as a cache flush plus an origin stampede at the moment you add capacity. Consistent hashing puts servers and URLs on one ring, so a new server steals only the arc in front of it.

You do not choose the city that answers you either. BGP anycast chooses, by policy and path length, not by latency, so it gives you a nearby point of presence and not reliably the nearest one. The CDN then names the building for you. Every Cloudflare-proxied site answers `/cdn-cgi/trace` with your address, the colo, the HTTP version and the TLS version, and the suffix of every `cf-ray` header prints the colo again. A colo is an IATA airport code, so BOM is Mumbai and IAD is Ashburn. Read it first when one user is slow or stale.

<Remember>

- A CDN is class 4's caching reverse proxy, rented in more than 300 cities.
- The cache is `proxy_cache`, the pool is `upstream keepalive`, the one address is anycast.
- `hash(url) % N` moves 90.9% of 100,000 URLs when 10 caches become 11.
- A capacity upgrade under modulo is a cache flush and an origin stampede at the worst moment.
- Consistent hashing, 160 points: 8.2% moved, 1.14 times the mean. The ideal is 9.1%.
- BGP anycast picks the colo, by policy and path length, not by latency.
- `/cdn-cgi/trace` prints the colo, and the suffix of `cf-ray` prints it on every response.

</Remember>

<KeyIdea id="two-defaults-you-did-not-choose" title="Two defaults you did not choose">

The edge decides eligibility from the file extension, and HTML and JSON are not on the list. The cache key is scheme plus host plus path plus query, and nothing else. Every default cache behaviour, good and catastrophic, follows from those two lines.

</KeyIdea>

A CDN asks two questions before it asks anything about freshness. Is this object eligible at all, and what is its key. Cloudflare answers the first from a list of 68 static file extensions. HTML and JSON are off that list, and so is any response with `Set-Cookie`, `private`, `no-store` or `max-age=0`. When the origin says nothing about freshness, the default edge TTL is 2 hours for 200 and 301, and 3 minutes for 404. The key leaves out cookies and almost every header, so two users with different cookies share one object. That is right for `app.css` and catastrophic for `/account`. `Vary`, from class 5, is how you tell the edge that two requests differ.

`cf-cache-status` is a small language with eight words, and the visual walks six of them. Two pairs repay care. REVALIDATED and EXPIRED both mean the TTL passed. REVALIDATED means the origin answered 304, so only headers crossed the long leg. EXPIRED means a full body crossed it. UPDATING and STALE both serve a stale body. UPDATING is planned, with a background refresh. STALE is unplanned, because the origin failed.

Pages got personal, so four tricks cache them anyway, and all four are one move: push the personal part outward so the shared part can be cached. Server-Side Includes, 1994, fill holes at the web server, before CGI existed. Edge-Side Includes, from Akamai and Oracle in 2001, fill the same holes at the point of presence. Ajax, about 2005, pushes them to the browser. Railgun, 2013 to 2024, sent only the changed bytes. The ESI bench is honest about the trade: origin bytes fall 97.9%, user bytes rise 0.06%, and origin requests climb from 100 to 201.

<Beyond source="Cloudflare cache docs, cache status response header, verified 2026-10-10" url="https://developers.cloudflare.com/cache/concepts/default-cache-behavior/">

Cloudflare documents more than eight values. NONE, UNKNOWN and DEFERRED also exist. The eight of this class are the teaching set, not the whole vocabulary.

</Beyond>

<Remember>

- Eligibility comes from the extension. HTML and JSON are off the list of 68, and so is anything with `Set-Cookie` or `private`.
- The default key is scheme plus host plus path plus query. Nothing else.
- Cookies are not in the key, so two users can share one object. `Vary` puts one back.
- HIT: fresh from the cache, origin never touched. MISS: eligible, absent, full body fetched.
- EXPIRED: TTL passed, full body came back. REVALIDATED: TTL passed, origin said 304.
- UPDATING: stale now, planned background refresh. STALE: the origin failed.
- DYNAMIC: never eligible, so the edge never looked. BYPASS: looked, then `no-store`.

</Remember>

<Picture breaks="a caretaker can look inside an envelope, and a cache deliberately does not, because reading every byte is what would make a cache slow.">

A noticeboard in a block of flats holds one copy of each notice, and the caretaker files each notice under its title alone. A prankster hands in a notice titled "Bin collection" with a false date inside, and for the next hour every resident who asks for it gets the prank. The second trick runs the other way. A resident posts a private bank letter under that same title, and the caretaker pins it to the public board, where the prankster reads it.

The title is the cache key, and the pages inside are the response. The first trick is cache poisoning, and the second is cache deception. Both times the caretaker filed by a name that did not describe the contents.

</Picture>

<KeyIdea id="the-key-is-the-name-of-the-answer" title="The key is the name of the answer">

Poisoning works when something that changed the answer is not in the name. Deception works when the name claims a type that the body does not have. One root cause: the cache key disagrees with reality.

</KeyIdea>

Cache poisoning, named by Kettle in 2018, needs one request. The origin reflects a header such as `X-Forwarded-Host` into the page, that header is not in the key, and the edge stores the hostile page under the plain URL. The lab window is 60 seconds, so one request each minute keeps the poison alive forever. The cost of the attack is the TTL, never the request count. Three fixes exist: drop the unkeyed header at the edge, add it to the key, or make the origin send `Vary` on it. Dropping is the defensible default, because it needs no help from the origin. Cache deception, named by Gil at Black Hat USA 2017, runs the attack backwards. The victim loads `/account/settings/fake.css`, the edge sees `.css`, and the private page lands where the attacker reads it. Its two fixes are the two halves of part 3.

Three mechanisms then move the same bytes faster. First, congestion control. CUBIC grows until a packet drops and then halves its window, which is the saw-tooth of class 2. On a long path, loss also comes from distance and random errors, so the halving is a mistake. BBR measures the real bottleneck bandwidth and the minimum round trip, then paces the sender to that rate. Only the sender changes, so a CDN that owns the sender on both legs can switch it on for you. Second, the warm pool. A connection opened by one worker can only be reused by that worker, so splitting the pool starves each part. Third, shielding. Without it, 300 edges each miss the same object and send 300 requests to your origin. Request collapsing stops that inside one city, and a tiered cache stops it across cities: never fetch the same object twice at once.

<Myth myth="BBR is the thing that makes the TCP saw-tooth.">

The saw-tooth belongs to a loss-based controller such as CUBIC, and class 2 drew it. BBR is the answer to it, not the cause of it. The class 6 source document stated this the wrong way round, and the deck corrects it on <SlideLink session={6} page={22} />. Getting the direction right is the point of the slide.

</Myth>

<Remember>

- Poisoning needs one request, and the TTL sets its cost, not the request rate.
- Three closes: drop the header, add it to the key, or send `Vary` on it. Drop it by default.
- Deception reverses it: a `.css` suffix on a private path stores the victim's page.
- Two closes for deception: stop trusting the extension, and honour `no-store`.
- One root cause for both: the cache key disagrees with reality.
- The saw-tooth belongs to CUBIC. BBR paces to a measured rate and ignores loss.
- Pool reuse: 0%, 45.3%, 82.7%, 98.7% for no pool, 32 pools, 8 pools, 1 shared pool.
- Most CDN-to-origin hops are still HTTP/1.1. Pooling, not multiplexing, saves the origin leg.
- Collapsing at one city and a tiered cache across cities: never fetch one object twice at once.

</Remember>

A festival puts one gate in front of the field. Everybody walks through that gate, so the gate becomes the place for everything else: a bag search, a counter that limits how many enter each minute, a wall of stewards for a crowd surge, and a ticket check. None of that was why the gate was built. The bag search works from a list of things it recognises, so a thing it has not seen walks through. And all of it is worthless if there is a hole in the fence round the back.

The gate is the edge. The bag search is the WAF, the counter is the rate limiter, the stewards are the flood absorption, and the ticket check is the API gateway. The hole in the fence is your origin, still answering anybody who reaches its address.

</Picture>

<KeyIdea id="the-perimeter-sits-where-traffic-converges" title="The perimeter sits wherever the traffic converges">

A CDN started as a cache. It became your security perimeter for one reason: a perimeter can only exist where all the traffic already goes. Speed got you to adopt it, and control is why you cannot leave.

</KeyIdea>

A WAF is a denylist of spellings. It reads the request text, decodes it by some amount, and matches a list of patterns. The lab shows two bypasses in four lines. `' OR 1=1 --` is blocked. `'/**/OR/**/1=1--` leaks 5 rows at the decode level, because SQL comments replace the spaces. And `' --` leaks 5 rows at every level, because it has no OR, no UNION and no digits to match. The arms race is normalisation against spelling, and you never win it outright. The bug still lives in your origin, and the fix is to bind the query parameters. A WAF is still a real layer, because it blocks the mass-scanned payloads and buys time.

A rate limit is only as good as how it counts, and the visual shows five answers to one phrase. Two failure modes matter. A fixed window lets 200 requests through in one blink at the boundary, and the Cloudflare free plan offers only that window. A leaky bucket with no burst refuses 97% of a normal page load, which is why `limit_req` in nginx has a burst parameter. The deployed answer is the sliding estimate: two counters for each client, and 0.003% wrong across 400 million requests. Geography then beats the algorithm, because counts are per data centre, so a botnet in 330 cities gets 330 budgets.

Flood absorption is the one job you could never do yourself. The record attack is 31.4 Tbps, reported by Cloudflare in December 2025, from 1 to 4 million devices for 35 seconds, and it was mitigated with no human in the loop. You cannot buy 31 Tbps of inbound capacity. A flood is won with more capacity than the attacker and enough cities that anycast splits it. The API gateway is the last job. The edge verifies the token, sets the identity header from the verified claims, and strips the headers your origin leaks. A token claiming `alg: none` is refused, because the token never picks the algorithm. All of it collapses if somebody reaches your origin directly, so a gateway is only real when the origin refuses every client except the edge.

- `' --` leaks at every normalisation level. Parameter binding in the origin is the fix.
- "100 per 10 seconds" has five meanings: 200, 100, 104, 109 and 10 at the boundary. The free plan offers only the 200.
- A leaky bucket with burst 0 refuses 97% of a normal page load.

</Remember>
