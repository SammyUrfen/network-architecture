Class 1 opened one socket and watched seven system calls carry bytes. Class 2 pulls the camera back and asks a bigger question: what carries those bytes, and who promises what?

The answer has one shape everywhere. A message goes inside an envelope, that envelope goes inside a bigger one, and each layer reads only its own envelope. The instructor walks the internet stack, then walks the phone network, which built the same onion in the 1970s. Then he types mail and file commands by hand, so you see real bytes.

Two questions run through the whole class. Where does a message end: at a length, or at a marker? And where does reliability live: at each hop, at the two ends, or inside the application? Every protocol here answers both, and the answers differ. That is the point.

**The five parts.** Part 1 builds the onion and shows why nothing above IP knows about copper, radio or space. Part 2 opens the phone network, which moved control off the voice path fifty years ago. Part 3 follows one text message and finds out why it holds 140 bytes. Part 4 takes TCP apart and counts the round trips that every modern protocol attacks. Part 5 types mail and file protocols by hand and lands on the two framing rules.

Each part gives you a picture, one key idea, a short list to hold, and three questions. The numbers table at the end holds every number that the quiz can ask for, with its conditions.

<Picture breaks="the sack stays the same for the whole trip. A real router throws the Ethernet frame away at each hop and builds a new one. Only the IP packet rides the whole way, with a lower TTL and a new checksum.">

You write a letter and seal it in an envelope. The post office puts your envelope in a sack that carries a city name, and a truck takes the sack. The driver never opens your envelope.

The letter is your data. Your envelope is the TCP header. The sack is the IP packet, and its city name is the destination address. The truck is the Ethernet frame on one link.

</Picture>

OSI names these layers. It is a committee model from 1984 that outlived almost everything built on it. Layers 7, 6 and 5 hold applications, encoding and sessions: HTTP, FTP, DNS and SMTP up top, then encryption and compression, then login state. Layer 4 is transport, layer 3 is the network, layer 2 is the link with Ethernet and MAC addresses, and layer 1 is cables and signals.

TCP and UDP share layer 4 and make opposite promises. TCP sets up a connection and delivers every byte in order, with flow control, congestion control and error recovery, and it pays in latency. UDP sends one datagram with no connection, no delivery promise and no order, and it is cheap. HTTP, FTP, SMTP and SSH take the first deal. DNS, voice, video and games take the second.

The byte budget falls out of the picture. A link carries 1500 bytes. Take 20 for IP and 20 for TCP, and 1460 bytes of your data are left: that is the MSS. Add 14 bytes of Ethernet header and a 4-byte check value, and one frame is 1518 bytes on the wire. To TCP, the request inside is a stream of bytes that it neither knows nor cares about.

<Myth claim="A 1500 MTU means a 1500-byte frame, or 1500 bytes of data.">

The 1500 is the largest IP packet. The frame around it is 1518 bytes, and your data inside it is 1460 bytes. Three different numbers, and the quiz can ask for any of them.

</Myth>

Change layer 1 and nothing above it notices. Starlink is a physical layer, and that is the whole point of having layers. The path runs laptop, Wi-Fi, dish, radio uplink, a satellite about 550 km up, a ground gateway, then the internet. The IP header arrives with the same addresses. Only the TTL, the checksum and any address that a NAT box rewrote have changed.

Radio does change how a link shares the air. On a wire a sender listens while it sends and detects a collision. A radio cannot, because its own signal drowns everything else. So Wi-Fi senses the channel, waits while it is busy, backs off for a random time, sends, and waits for an ACK. It therefore acknowledges each frame at layer 2, long before TCP at layer 4 learns that anything happened. The deck puts about 2 ms of contention before a frame leaves, and it gives no conditions for that number.

<KeyIdea id="a-header-wraps-an-opaque-body" title="A header wraps a body that it never reads">

Each layer adds its own header and treats everything inside as bytes it must not touch. A router reads the IP header and forwards the packet, and it never opens the TCP header. Change the bottom layer to radio or to a satellite, and the layers above do not change at all.

</KeyIdea>

<Picture breaks="the headset line trusts anyone who plugs in. SS7 joins hundreds of operators, and it trusts any point code on the line.">

A theatre show has two channels. The actors speak on stage, and the crew gets cues. In the old theatre the crew heard its cues from the stage, so anybody in the audience who shouted "lights out" turned the lights out. The new theatre gives the crew a private headset line.

The stage is the voice circuit. The cues are the signalling. The shout is the blue box, a tone box that seized a trunk for free. The headset line is SS7.

</Picture>

Before SS7, signalling travelled inside the voice channel. A 2600 Hz tone meant "this trunk is idle", and other tones carried the digits. Whoever makes the sound makes the decision, so a box that played the right tones got free calls. From about 1975 SS7 fixes that: take the signalling out of the voice channel. The voice then rides a 64 kbps circuit, and control rides a separate packet network that the caller never touches. That is the control plane and the data plane, split apart, and every network you build still does it.

SS7 is a whole stack, so the fair comparison is stack against stack, never SS7 against TCP alone. MTP1 is a circuit on an E1 or T1 line. MTP2 is the link layer: it numbers each signal unit, checks a CRC-16 and asks for a bad unit again. MTP3 routes by point code. Above them, SCCP addresses subsystems and phone numbers, TCAP carries dialogues, and MAP or ISUP does the application work.

<KeyIdea id="control-left-the-voice-path" title="Control left the voice path, and never came back">

SS7 moved call setup onto its own network, so the voice channel carries only voice. The split killed the blue box, because the caller can no longer reach the control channel. The same instinct returns in SIP, which keeps media off the proxy, and in FTP, which opens a second connection for each file.

</KeyIdea>

The 5G version of a call is the same ladder in text over IP. SIP sends an INVITE with a media offer through a proxy, and 100 Trying, 180 Ringing and 200 OK come back. The final ACK goes end to end, past the proxy, and the audio flows between the phones as RTP over UDP. BYE and 200 OK end the call. Signalling is text, media is RTP, and the two take separate paths.

<Beyond source="RFC 3261 §21">

SIP is "consistent with, and extends" the HTTP codes. It does not copy them unchanged. HTTP/1.1 defines 100 Continue and 200 OK, but 180 Ringing exists only in SIP, and SIP adds a whole 6xx class of its own.

</Beyond>

Now the reliability question. SS7 makes every hop reliable: MTP2 numbers each unit with a 7-bit number that wraps at 128, and the far end of that one link asks for it again. TCP makes only the two endpoints reliable, with a 32-bit number that counts bytes. Congestion control splits the same way, hop by hop in MTP3 against end to end in TCP. Security fails the same way in both: SS7 trusts a point code and checks nothing else, and TCP has none of its own and leans on TLS above it. Both stacks solve the same eleven problems. They only disagree about which layer owns each one.

<Picture breaks="a real shirt folds to any size. A GSM character is exactly 7 bits, and a few characters such as the curly brace or the euro sign need an escape and cost 14 bits.">

A locker holds 272 units. The house rules say a bag may hold 200. Your suitcase is 140, so the suitcase and its labels fit inside one locker with room left.

The locker is one SS7 signal unit. The bag is the MAP field that carries user data. The suitcase is the text itself. Packing is a trick with shirts: each shirt takes 7 slots instead of 8, so 160 shirts fit where 140 fit before.

</Picture>

An SMS nests like a web request, fifty years apart. MTP2 holds MTP3, which holds SCCP, which holds TCAP, which holds MAP, which holds the message. The MAP invoke carries opCode 46, mo-ForwardSM, which means a phone sends a text. Inside it sits the SMS-SUBMIT unit, with the message type, a reference number, the destination number, the alphabet setting, a validity time and the user data length.

Three limits stack, and the smallest one wins. One signal unit carries at most 272 octets. The MAP user data field holds about 200. The text field, TP-UD, holds 140. So an SMS is 140 bytes because a 1980s signalling packet caps at 272. That choice buys something real: the whole operation fits in one signal unit, so nothing splits, nothing reassembles and nobody keeps state. SMS ran on 1980s hardware for that reason, and it still works when the data network is down.

<Beyond source="ITU-T Q.703 and Q.704">

The SIF cap is 272 octets, and 4 of those go to the routing label, so 268 are left. Two slides say 273 instead. The 273 is the SIF plus the one service octet in front of it, so both numbers describe real things. Learn 272 for the exam.

</Beyond>

<KeyIdea id="the-smallest-limit-wins" title="Three limits stack, and the smallest one wins">

The signal unit allows 272 octets, the MAP user data field allows about 200, and the text field allows 140. Headers eat part of each larger number, so the 140 is the one you feel. Every layered design has a limit like this, and the tightest one always reaches your product.

</KeyIdea>

Now the arithmetic. 140 octets are 1120 bits, a GSM 7-bit character takes 7 bits, and 1120 divided by 7 is exactly 160. Packing pushes the septets into one bit stream and then cuts the stream every 8 bits, so after the first character the boundaries stop lining up. That is why "Hello World!" reads as 11 strange octets: 12 characters are 84 bits, and 84 bits travel as 11 octets with 4 zero bits of padding. Switch to UCS-2 for an emoji or a non-Latin script and each character costs 16 bits, so the same 140 octets hold only 70. One emoji can cost you a second message.

<Myth claim="An SMS holds 140 characters.">

It holds 140 octets. How many characters that is depends on the alphabet: 160 with GSM 7-bit, and 70 with UCS-2. The limit is a size in bytes, never a count of characters.

</Myth>

Delivery is a database lookup wrapped in a queue, and the order of the steps is a favourite exam trap. Phone A sends SMS-SUBMIT to the switch that serves it. That switch sends MAP mo-ForwardSM to the SMSC. The SMSC stores the message and answers with a TCAP END, which means accepted and queued. Only then does it ask the HLR, with sendRoutingInfoForSM, where the other phone is now. The HLR returns the IMSI and the address of the switch that serves phone B. The SMSC sends mt-ForwardSM with an SMS-DELIVER there, and that switch delivers over the air and returns the result. A status report reaches phone A only if the sender asked for one. So accepted and delivered are two different events, and the END proves only the first.

<Picture breaks="page numbers count pages. TCP counts bytes, and a SYN or a FIN takes one number without carrying any byte at all.">

Two friends share a notebook and agree where each one starts writing. "I start at page 1000." "Got it, so I expect 1001 next. I start at page 5000." "Got it, I expect 5001 next." To stop, one says "I have nothing more to say", and the other says "OK" and keeps talking for a while. Later the second one says "now I stop too", and gets an "OK" back.

Each start number is a sequence number, and each "got it" is an ack. Four sentences close the notebook, because each friend stops at a different time.

</Picture>

TCP opens with three messages because each direction needs its own start number confirmed. The client SYN announces its number. The server SYN-ACK acks that number and announces its own, so two messages merge into one. The client ACK confirms the server number. SYN and FIN each consume one sequence number although they carry no data, which is why an ack of 1001 answers a SYN with seq 1000, and why nobody can confuse a bare SYN with an old copy of one.

Start numbers used to be predictable, and that was a real hole. Guess the next one and you can inject forged segments blind, which is the Mitnick attack. RFC 6528 made the start number a keyed hash of the four-tuple plus a timer.

The gap between message 1 and message 3 is the target of a SYN flood. Spoofed SYNs cost the attacker one packet each and cost the server one half-open entry until a timeout fires. Fill that table and real clients get refused. SYN cookies, from 1996, remove the table. The server stores nothing and hides the details inside its own start number: a keyed hash of the four-tuple, a coarse clock and the MSS. The client hands that number back plus one, and the server rebuilds the connection from that one packet. The cost is space, because 32 bits leave no room for window scaling, SACK or timestamps. So cookies switch on under pressure and not all the time.

<Myth claim="A SYN flood fills the accept queue.">

It fills the half-open entries between message 1 and message 3. The attacker never sends message 3, so no handshake ever finishes and nothing reaches the accept queue. Both queues carry the same nickname, "backlog", which is why this trips people up.

</Myth>

Closing takes four segments because each direction closes on its own. One side sends FIN and the other acks it, and that other side can keep sending for as long as it likes. Its own FIN waits for its own call to close. Two of these states answer real production questions. Sockets stuck in CLOSE_WAIT almost always mean an application that forgot to call close: look at your code, not your switch. TIME_WAIT on a listening address is why a server restart says "address already in use", which is the whole reason SO_REUSEADDR exists.

<Beyond source="RFC 9293 §3.6 and §3.4.2">

The deck says that FIN and ACK cannot share a segment. They can. A passive closer whose application closes at once sends its FIN together with the ACK, so a three-segment close is common in real captures. Learn four as the general case. Also, MSL is 2 minutes in the spec, which makes TIME_WAIT 4 minutes, while Linux uses 60 seconds.

</Beyond>

<KeyIdea id="each-direction-closes-on-its-own" title="Each direction closes on its own">

Open needs three messages, because two start numbers need acks and one message carries both jobs. Close needs four, because the two directions stop at different times. Only the side that closes first waits in TIME_WAIT, and that side can be the server.

</KeyIdea>

BBR changed the congestion question in 2016. Reno and CUBIC grow the window every round trip until something drops, then cut back and grow again. They need loss to make progress. On a fat, long or lossy path that is exactly wrong, because a radio or a satellite link drops packets for reasons that have nothing to do with a full queue. BBR stops treating loss as the signal. It measures the bottleneck bandwidth and the round-trip propagation time, then sends at the rate the path carries and keeps the queue near empty.

<Beyond source="Google Cloud blog, 2017-07-21, and RFC 9438 §4.6">

The deck says "up to 2,000×". The Google Cloud post reports 2,700×, and it gives the conditions: CUBIC reached 3.3 Mbps and BBR reached 9,100 Mbps on 10 GbE, with 100 ms RTT and 1% loss. Quote the number with its conditions or not at all. The deck also says that Reno and CUBIC both halve on loss. Reno halves, and CUBIC cuts to 0.7.

</Beyond>

Every round trip before your first byte of data is a tax. As the deck draws it, plain TCP costs 1 round trip, TCP with TLS 1.2 costs 3, TCP with TLS 1.3 costs 2, TCP Fast Open costs 1, and QUIC costs 1. Fast Open lets a returning client put data in the SYN itself. QUIC folds the transport setup and the crypto setup into one round trip. You can buy bandwidth, but you cannot buy round trips: Mumbai to Virginia is about 190 ms whatever you pay.

UDP is TCP with every promise removed. Its header is 8 bytes in all, two ports plus a length plus a checksum, and the payload reaches 65,507 bytes over IPv4. Zoom, WebRTC, game servers, DNS and real-time streams take that deal, because a resent audio frame arrives after its play time. A gap is the correct result.

QUIC then rebuilds TCP inside UDP, in userspace, because UDP is 8 bytes that the kernel and the middleboxes leave alone. HTTP/2 needs a separate TLS handshake on one ordered TCP stream, while HTTP/3 runs on QUIC, where streams and TLS 1.3 share one handshake. One lost packet stalls every HTTP/2 stream over TCP, because TCP hands bytes up in order. QUIC gives each stream its own sequence space, so only the stream that lost a packet waits. TCP also lives in the kernel, so a change needs an operating system update for the whole planet, while QUIC ships in the browser and can change on Tuesday. That answers the reliability question a third time: reliability has an address, and QUIC put it in userspace.

<Picture breaks="a paper letter keeps one envelope. Each mail relay writes a new envelope and adds a line to the letter itself. SMTP also cannot change its end marker, so the sender must protect every body line that starts with a dot.">

The post office reads only the envelope. Inside it you can sign the letter with any name you like, and nobody checks. A radio operator ends a turn with a set phrase, so any message that holds that phrase must be marked, or the listener stops early.

The envelope is the pair MAIL FROM and RCPT TO. The letter is the headers and the body. The set phrase is the line that holds only a dot.

</Picture>

SMTP, POP and FTP are text, so you can be the client with a terminal. The S in SMTP means Simple and never Secure. The ports matter: 25 relays mail from server to server, 587 is submission with STARTTLS and is the one a mail client should use, 465 is submission with TLS from the first byte, 110 is POP3 and 143 is IMAP. SMTP stayed text because each relay reads the message, rewrites the envelope and adds its own headers. A header that nobody understands breaks nothing, which is why forty years of extensions bolted on cleanly.

That openness is also the hole. MAIL FROM is whatever you type, and the base protocol authenticates nothing at all. SPF, DKIM and DMARC are later patches that ask what SMTP never asked: is this sender allowed to say that? Keep the envelope and the letter apart in your head. MAIL FROM and RCPT TO decide delivery, while the From: and To: headers inside DATA are text that nothing checks.

The session shape is fixed: a 220 greeting, EHLO with a 250 and the extension list, MAIL FROM and RCPT TO each with a 250, DATA with a 354, then the headers, one blank line and the body, then a line that holds only a dot, and 221 after QUIT. Two traps live there. The blank line ends the headers, not the message. And a body line that starts with a dot needs one extra dot, or the server ends your message early.

A text protocol cannot carry arbitrary bytes, so images travel as base64. Base64 takes 3 bytes, which are 24 bits, and regroups them as four 6-bit values, so the bytes of "Man" become TWFu. The wire cost is 4 characters for every 3 bytes, about 33%, and the MIME rules cap a line at 76 characters. The alphabet is A to Z, a to z, 0 to 9, plus and slash, with the equals sign as padding, and every mail system passes it unchanged. An attachment uses multipart/mixed, which names a boundary: each part starts with two hyphens and that boundary, and carries its own content type. Pick a boundary that cannot appear in the body. That is the delimiter idea again, with the same escaping problem.

Reading mail makes state placement the design question. POP is four verbs: STAT for the count and size, LIST for each size, RETR for one whole message, and DELE to mark one, which QUIT then applies. USER and PASS cross the wire in clear text, which is why POP3S on port 995 exists. The real difference is not the verbs. POP keeps mail on your device and treats the server as a spool to empty, so a second device sees nothing. IMAP keeps mail on the server and gives the client a cache, so every device sees the same mailbox. Read state follows: POP tracks it locally, and IMAP stores it as a server flag such as \\Seen. POP has one inbox and local search, while IMAP has folders over the wire, SEARCH on the server and IDLE that pushes new mail. POP RETR returns the whole message, while IMAP FETCH BODY[1] returns one MIME part. Everything else follows from one choice: POP puts the state on your machine, and IMAP puts it on the server.

<KeyIdea id="length-or-delimiter-nothing-else" title="Length or delimiter. There is no third option.">

Every protocol marks the end of a message with a length or with a marker. SMS chose a length, SMTP chose a lone dot, and IMAP uses both. A delimiter is cheap and it streams, but you must escape it or your content becomes protocol. A length needs no escaping, but you must know the size before you start.

</KeyIdea>

The costs are worth stating plainly, because the assignment and the project both turn on them. A delimiter lets you send before you know the size, and the reader scans every byte: SMTP uses a lone dot, HTTP/1.1 uses a blank line after the headers, and MIME uses a boundary. Forget the escape and a body line ends the message early, and the rest of your text reads as commands. A length prefix means the reader never scans the payload, so no byte inside can be dangerous: SMS sends TP-UDL, HTTP sends Content-Length, and Protobuf sends a varint. The price is knowing the size first, which is what chunked encoding avoids. Some protocols use both, such as IMAP literals and HTTP/2 frames, and request smuggling lives in the gap where two parsers disagree.

<Beyond source="ITU-T Q.703 §2.3.2 and RFC 1939 §3">

Two columns on the framing slide need a correction. MTP2 sits under "length", but it ends each unit at a flag byte and inserts a zero bit after five ones, so the flag cannot appear inside. That is a delimiter with an escape. POP3 sits under "both", but nothing frames a POP message by length: CRLF ends each line, and a lone dot ends a multi-line reply.

</Beyond>

FTP answers the same question with a connection instead of a rule. It refuses to mix commands and data: one connection holds the conversation, and a new connection carries each file. The 1971 design is active mode, where control goes to port 21 and the server dials back from port 20, which every NAT box and firewall now blocks. Passive mode fixes it. The client sends PASV, the server opens a listening port and replies 227 with six numbers, and the client connects there. So the client opens both connections, the only shape NAT allows, and each transfer needs a new PASV.

Decoding that reply is the byte-order lesson in disguise. In (127,0,0,1,117,48) the first four numbers give the address and the last two give the port, high byte first: 117 times 256 plus 48 is 30000. The reply is text, so a 16-bit port ships as two 8-bit halves in network byte order. That is htons done by hand. The class demo also pins the passive ports to a small range, which real servers do to keep the firewall rule finite, and sets the address in the 227 reply, because a server inside a container does not know the address a client can reach.
